A Robust Biometric Authentication System
for Handheld Electronic Devices
by Intelligently Combining 3D Finger
Motions and Cerebral Responses
Santosh K. Behera
, Pradeep Kumar, Debi P. Dogra
, Member, IEEE, and Partha P. Roy, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Rapid advancement in sensor technology through
miniaturization of electronic components has enabled the con-
sumer electronic (CE) research community including the manu-
facturers to embed various utility sensors into handheld devices.
In addition to traditional sensors such as Inertial Measurement
Unit (IMU), camera, ﬁngerprint or proximity, futuristic sen-
sors such as Electroencephalogram (EEG) or Electromyography
(EMG) are also being included in the next-generation CE devices.
Air or touch signature-based authentication systems are common
in modern CE devices. However, cerebral activities clubbed with
gestures will certainly enhance the security of such authentication
systems. This can help consumers from being the victims of shoul-
der surﬁng attacks. In this article, a new method is proposed to
verify air signatures by analyzing ﬁnger movements and cerebral
activities together with the help of sensors in next-generation CE
devices. Signatures are ﬁrst spotted by analyzing 3D geometrical
features of the ﬁnger movement during the signing. Concurrent
EEG responses are then analyzed for the veriﬁcation. Hidden
Markov Model (HMM) and Random Forest (RF) classiﬁers have
been used to train the system. Experiments reveal that EEG sig-
nals are highly correlated with the ﬁnger movements during air
signatures even in the presence of motion artifacts. Therefore,
false-positive rates have signiﬁcantly reduced as compared to the
existing tracking-based methods. Veriﬁcation accuracy as high as
95.5% (HMM) and 98.5% (RF) have been recorded when tested
on our dataset.
Index Terms—Biometric authentication, air signature, signa-
ture spotting, signature veriﬁcation.

Typical device authentication processes use biometric traits
or features to verify whether a person is authorized to access
the device or not. Air signature/gesture in 3D is a well-known

writing has the advantage of touch-less or paper-less authen-

during air writing, enabling more security because it is difﬁ-
cult for the intruders to ﬁnd the exact location of the signature.
While signing in the air, users can randomly scribble before
and/or after the actual signature. This makes it difﬁcult to
localize the signature. However, existing gesture-based authen-
tication systems are prone to shoulder surﬁng attacks. Thus,
incorporating additional information such as cerebral activities
(through EEG responses) in the feature vector can make such
systems robust to shoulder surﬁng attacks.
A. Motivation and Contributions
Air signatures are becoming very popular for biometric
authentication. However, such systems are subject to identify
theft if a professional forger observes or video-records the
signing process. Therefore, we need to enhance the robust-
ness of the signing process. One way can be to ask the signer
to put the signature within a random writing. This will ensure
that the forger does not get information about the start and end
points of the actual signature. During the veriﬁcation, ﬁrst we
need to localize or spot the signature in the random writing,
followed by the actual veriﬁcation. In other words, spotting is
a problem that works at a coarser level and it does not defy
the basic objective of biometric authentication. It can be stated
that, despite erroneous spotting, veriﬁcation can be achieved
with higher accuracy if the feature-set is robust. Moreover,

II. RELATED WORK
Kim and Hong [2] proposed a new multimodal biomet-
ric authentication approach using teeth image and voice as
biometric traits to protect private information stored in the
mobile devices. The method has an ERR of 2.13% over 1000
images. Liu and Song [3] proposed a real-time embedded
ﬁnger-vein recognition system for authentication on mobile
devices which takes about 0.8 seconds to verify one input
ﬁnger-vein sample with an EER rate of 0.07% over a database
of 100 subjects. Galka et al. [4] presented the concept of the
embedded solution for voice biometric access system as well
as automatic speech processing methods keeping performance
optimization methods as one of the main concern. To over-
come various challenges associated with iris acquisition on
handheld imaging devices, Thavalengal et al. [5] suggested
potential solutions for unconstrained acquisition using smart-
phones. Bao et al. [6] designed hand-gesture recognition
network that can classify seven sorts of hand gestures in a
user-independent manner and on real time. Lee [7] discussed
security issue and advancement for human shoulder-surﬁng

ferent digit orderings one for the user close to the device and
another for the attacker who is looking at the device from
a bigger distance. Sun et al. [9] proposed an authentication

to overcome the weakness of humans for choosing a bad

camera-based attacks. Zhu et al. [10] tried to explore AI prob-
lems and captcha as a graphical password (CaRP) scheme to
improving online security that are resistant to online guessing
attacks, relay attacks and shoulder-surng attacks and image

Signatures can be imitated by impostee ers if they are

tures secure, people may be encouraged to sign in the air
randomly. However, random writing makes a typical biomet-

extremely important. In this article, a new approach has been
proposed to spot signatures in random air writing and then
verify by analyzing the EEG responses. As EEG response is
unique, it can help to improve the robustness. Also, the signal
can be captured conveniently using low-cost commercial sen-
sors [39]. Therefore, such sensors are already in use with the
handheld devices [40], [41].

have been collected using the process discussed in Section III.

tuning. Additionally, Random forests use the bagging tech-
nique which is based on averaging multiple deep decision

VI. SPOTTING AND VERIFICATION OF SIGNATURES
The methodology is divided into two phases, namely signa-
ture spotting and signature veriﬁcation as shown in the Fig. 6.
In the ﬁrst phase, probable locations of signatures (within ran-
dom writing) are obtained using 3D global features extracted
from the ﬁnger movement data acquired through motion sensor
interface. A sliding window-based method has been consid-
ered to spot the possible locations of the signature within a
random writing. In the next phase, the EEG data correspond-
ing to the spotted locations are tested using HMM and RF for
veriﬁcation.

spotting.

B. Veriﬁcation of Signatures
Since the spotted window may not fully contain the signa-
ture, it often becomes difﬁcult to verify the signature using the
geometrical 3D features of that window. Therefore, the brain
signals have been used for veriﬁcation to reduce false rejec-
tion rates. EEG signals may be corrupted by motion artefacts
due to the movement of the body parts during air signa-
ture. Therefore, raw EEG signals are smoothed using Savitzky
Golay (S-Golay) ﬁlter. After spotting using geometrical fea-
tures, the EEG signals of the corresponding window are used
in veriﬁcation.
• Veriﬁcation Using HMM: For EEG, gamma features are
trained using HMM classiﬁer. We created HMM mod-
els for all users using Baum-Welch training algorithm.
θ = (T, O, π) represents each HMM model, where T
denotes state transition matrix, O denotes observation
matrix, and π denotes initial state probabilities [29], [34].
After training, a test window (spotted EEG window) is
compared with all the models and measure probability
of matching, i.e., likelihood. The model which gives the
largest likelihood value, is assumed to be the user to
whom the signature possibly belongs.
• Veriﬁcation Using RF: The feature vector deﬁned in (5) is
then used for classiﬁcation of a typical input EEG frame
corresponding to the spotted signatures. A RF-based clas-
siﬁer has been used for classiﬁcation. Random Forest is
represented as the collection of classiﬁcation trees, where
each decision tree assigns the input data to one of the
assigned classes [45].
VII. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS
This section describes the data as well as presents the results
of spotting and veriﬁcation. To the best of our knowledge, such
multi-sensor datasets are not publicly available. Therefore, in
our laboratory environment, we created a multi-sensor dataset
involving 20 users with the help of motion and EEG sensors.
We tested our approach using this dataset which we made
available to the researchers.1
A. Dataset Description
There is no publicly available multi-sensor dataset for the
present task. Therefore, we made a setup in our lab environ-

We involved 12 male and 8 female participants in the data

F. Comparative Analysis
Since no work is available in the literature that uses ﬁngertip
motion and EEG responses together, we performed a compar-
ison with the existing techniques that use motion sensors for
air signature veriﬁcation. Recently Guerra-Segura et al. [46]
proposed an air-signature veriﬁcation system using the same
type of motion sensor used in our work. The authors used APIs
of the sensor for extracting six types of features by dividing
the time-series data into multiple segments. The veriﬁcation
was done using SVM with EERs of 0.25 and 1.25, respec-
tively on random and expert forgers. However, in their study,
all the forgeries were attempted by two imposters only and
the veriﬁcation was not attempted using any temporal clas-
siﬁers. Likewise, the authors in [47] developed a sequential
CNN architecture for geometric features extracted from air
signatures and reported an average veriﬁcation accuracy of
95%. We recorded accuracy as high as 95.5% and 98.5%,
respectively using HMM and RF classiﬁers. We observed that
our proposed multi-modal setup performs better in compari-
son to the aforementioned uni-modal systems. A comparison
with other techniques is presented in Table III.

VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Modern sensor technology is enriching various research
areas including human-computer interface and biometric.
Moreover, the integration of these sensors with the next-
generation handheld devices is being adopted at a rapid speed.
This article proposes a method to spot air signatures scribbled
within the random writings recorded through portable motion
sensors embedded in CE devices and verify these signatures
by analyzing corresponding EEG responses. EEG responses
are recorded using easy-to-use low-cost sensors attached with
such devices. The classiﬁcation has been performed using
HMM and RF classiﬁers. In the ﬁrst phase, the signatures
are spotted using a heuristic that relies on the 3D geomet-
rical features extracted from the raw data captured through
the motion sensor attached with the device. In the next phase,
spotted signatures are veriﬁed using HMM and RF classiﬁers.
Both classiﬁers perform satisfactorily with RF has a slight
edge over the HMM. The method can be highly effective in
enhancing the capabilities of typical CE setups. In the future,
advanced sequential learning methods such as temporal CNNs
or LSTMs can be used for improving the veriﬁcation results.
